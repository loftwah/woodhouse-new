# UNSLOP

This is how the factory works. A generic generated-looking website fails the product mission.

- No generic SaaS dashboard, card soup or analytics wallpaper.
- No glowing AI orb, purple-blue gradient, gratuitous glass or decorative charts.
- No fake terminal, filler numbers, invented live events or guessed completion percentages.
- No rainbow statuses. Use labelled states and shape as well as colour.
- No pile of rounded rectangles around information that already has a clear hierarchy.
- No icon in place of plain language.
- No jokes that substitute for useful product design.
- No desktop layout stretched across mobile or mobile layout with its substance hidden.
- No hover-only controls, surprise motion or large client-side framework for static reading.
- No internal vocabulary in introductory copy. A visitor is not asked to understand "dossier", "qualification", "proof boundary" or "admitted project" before they can play, listen or try something.
- No development status presented as the product's explanation. A visitor meets what the thing is and what they can do with it first, in words they would use themselves.
- No “autonomous”, “qualified”, “production verified” or “physically proven” without evidence for that specific claim.
- No private conversation or repository payload in public assets.
- Do not explain internal fallback, retry, cache or recovery behavior in copy written for readers. State the useful source, status or limitation from the reader's point of view.

The product is not optimised to look like a portfolio. It is designed to be Woodhouse.

## Writing that earns a reader's time

Reviewed 18 October 2026. The observed failure is treating a technically accurate release summary
as a compelling project story. An article needs an argument, a useful example and editorial judgement.

- Start with the thing a reader can understand or inspect. Choose an angle specific to this project;
  swapping the product name should break the article.
- Make a claim with consequences. Explain the mechanism and the cost of the choice. Keep opinion
  recognisable as opinion, and put a material limitation beside the claim it limits.
- Remove empty significance, unnamed experts, invented experience and unsupported comparisons.
  Avoid synonym cycling, compulsory threes, repeated "not X, but Y" constructions, canned rhetorical
  questions and paragraphs that announce what the article will explain.
- Read for manufactured swagger too: profanity, sentence fragments and a slogan every few lines
  can be as empty as corporate enthusiasm. Let sentence length follow the thought. Keep a strong
  judgement when the evidence supports it.
- Edit the argument before polishing the words. Read the draft aloud, check its factual claims
  against current sources, then cut the passages that repeat the point. No word blacklist or
  detector score can establish authorship or substitute for an edit.
- Finish with an action the reader can actually take. Preserve the project's current availability;
  a prototype can earn a good article without becoming a launch or a customer success story.

### Brief, draft, edit

Use this routine for articles and scale it down for shorter copy. It needs no separate planning
document, fixed article template, word-count target or automated writing score.

Before drafting, decide who is reading, what they should understand or be able to do afterwards,
and which defensible claim makes this project worth their time. Find the strongest current example
and its sources. If the evidence changes the angle, change the angle before writing the headline.

Build the draft around that example. Explain what happens, why the choice matters and what it
costs. Give necessary background where the reader needs it. Paragraphs and headings should follow
the argument; different projects need different structures. Preserve the useful distinction between
a product's enduring purpose and its dated development state.

Before publication, make these editing passes:

1. **Challenge the facts.** Find the weakest claim. Check names, dates, versions, ownership and
   availability against current primary sources. Distinguish a demonstration from general capability
   and a possible audience from proven demand. Correct or remove an unsupported claim; hedging and
   a citation do not repair it.
2. **Challenge the argument.** Make the stakes and mechanism understandable. Address the strongest
   relevant objection or cost. Cut paragraphs that repeat the premise, narrate the writing process
   or congratulate the product without showing anything new.
3. **Listen to the voice.** Read the prose as spoken language. Prefer concrete nouns, active verbs
   and Australian English. Vary sentence length with the thought. Keep personality and a supported
   judgement; remove canned enthusiasm, forced slang and theatrical fragments. Do not invent an
   experience, quote or personal memory to make the writing sound human.
4. **Check the invitation.** The headline and deck must promise what the article delivers. End on
   the reader's useful next action or a conclusion the argument earns. Check captions, link labels
   and share text against that same promise. Verify actual destinations and material limitations
   through the existing publication path; passing structural checks does not establish good writing.

### Lessons from the App Composer edit

Reviewed 18 October 2026. Keep these distinctions in future project stories:

- A source-controlled fictional postcard gave the independence argument something a reader could
  inspect. It did not establish unfamiliar-creator onboarding or a completed migration.
- An app operator controlling publication does not establish that they own a contributor's photo
  or have a legal right to redistribute it. Name the actor and the precise control involved.
- Explaining the cost of separate contracts and deployments made the case more credible. Keep a
  consequential tradeoff even when writing confidently.
- The concrete downloaded-copy limit did its job. Commentary praising the promise's testability
  and the article's persuasiveness added nothing; the edit removed it and ended on an actual action.

The research informs these choices; it does not supply a universal template. These are close-reading
judgements about why the articles work, not measured explanations of their reach:

- [Paul Graham's Maker's Schedule, Manager's Schedule](https://paulgraham.com/makersschedule.html)
  gives a familiar frustration a useful name, then explains the cost of an interruption through a
  concrete working day. Borrow the explanatory precision, not the author's personal experience.
- [Joel Spolsky's Things You Should Never Do, Part I](https://www.joelonsoftware.com/2000/04/06/things-you-should-never-do-part-i/)
  makes a forceful argument through a named failure and shows how apparently messy code preserves
  hard-won knowledge. Borrow the causal detail; challenge the absolute claim where the evidence differs.
- [Dan Luu's In defense of simple architectures](https://danluu.com/simple-architectures/)
  earns its position with operational choices, admitted mistakes and exceptions. Give a technical
  reader enough detail to disagree intelligently.

[Measuring AI "Slop" in Text](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.19163) treats quality through interpretable
dimensions and reports subjectivity in binary judgements. [Wikipedia's editing observations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing)
are useful warning patterns for revision. Neither makes a punctuation mark proof of AI authorship.
Our editorial concern is writing that wastes attention through generic claims, repetition or missing
substance. AI assistance does not excuse that failure, and disguising assistance does not fix it.
